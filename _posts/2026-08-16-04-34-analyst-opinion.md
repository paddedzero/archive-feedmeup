### Technical Analysis: What's Really Happening

### The Mechanic: What's Actually Happening

The AppSec landscape of mid-2026 is defined by a sobering realization: we have traded the "noisy" vulnerabilities of the past for a more insidious, quiet class of architectural failures. For years, we chased buffer overflows and SQL injections. Today, as the latest OWASP strategic shift suggests, the industry is moving away from "vulnerability hunting" and toward "systemic resilience." The data from the recent May 2026 roundup reveals three distinct mechanical shifts that every CISO needs to parse.

First, let’s talk about the **Rust migration**. The "rewrite it in Rust" movement has moved out of the experimental phase and into the audit phase. The results are fascinating, if not entirely what the evangelists promised. While we are seeing a near-total elimination of memory-safety vulnerabilities in core modules—effectively killing off the "low-hanging fruit" for remote code execution—we are seeing a spike in **Logic-State Collisions**. Developers, so focused on satisfying the Rust borrow checker, are inadvertently creating complex state machines that are syntactically perfect but logically flawed. We’ve traded the "crash" for the "silent bypass."

Second, we are witnessing the emergence of **Advanced Repudiation Threats**. In the 2024 era, "Repudiation" was the forgotten stepchild of the STRIDE model. In 2026, it is the frontline. With the ubiquity of generative identity—AI that can mimic a developer’s coding style, commit patterns, and even their voice in "quick syncs"—the fundamental concept of the audit log is under siege. We are seeing attackers compromise developer environments and then use local LLMs to generate "human-like" activity that masks malicious commits. When the breach is discovered, the forensic trail is so poisoned with realistic "slop" that the legal department cannot definitively prove *who* or *what* initiated the action.

Finally, there is the **AI Feedback Loop**, or what researchers are calling "Model Inbreeding." The May roundup highlights that AppSec scanners are now drowning in "AI Slop"—code that was generated by an LLM, trained on code generated by a previous LLM, which was itself trained on a hallucination. This has created a "vulnerability drift" where security tools are looking for patterns that no longer exist, while missing new, non-deterministic bugs that look like valid business logic but function as backdoors.

### The "So What?": Why This Matters

This isn't just another set of patches to deploy; this is a fundamental erosion of the **Security Source of Truth**. If you cannot trust your logs (Repudiation), and you cannot trust the "memory-safe" code to be logically sound (The Rust Paradox), your entire risk posture shifts from "Preventative" to "Speculative."

The "So What" here is the **Death of the Deterministic Security Model**. For thirty years, security was a math problem: *If X input occurs, Y filter blocks it.* In the world of AI-generated slop and logic-heavy Rust rewrites, security is becoming a probabilistic nightmare. We are seeing a 40% increase in "Ghost Incidents"—breaches where no specific CVE was exploited, but rather a series of "safe" logical operations were chained together by an adversary who understood the system better than the developers who "prompted" it into existence.

Furthermore, the OWASP strategic pivot is a signal that the old "Top 10" framework is dead. It was built for an era of monolithic web apps. In 2026, our applications are ephemeral, polyglot, and increasingly autonomous. If your security architecture is still optimized for the 2021 OWASP Top 10, you are essentially bringing a knife to a drone fight. You are protecting against injection while the attacker is simply convincing your AI-orchestrator that the malicious payload is a "necessary configuration update."

The economic impact is also surfacing. The "Rust Rewrites" are showing a massive ROI in terms of long-term stability, but the initial "Security Debt" incurred during the migration is staggering. Companies are finding that they’ve secured their memory but left their API logic wide open because the senior talent was too busy fighting the compiler to review the architecture.

### Strategic Defense: What To Do About It

We need to stop treating AppSec as a gatekeeping function and start treating it as an **Attestation and Observability** function. If we can't prevent the "slop," we must be able to prove its origin.

#### 1. Immediate Actions (Tactical Response)

*   **Implement Cryptographic Commit Signing (FIDO3-backed):** Move beyond simple GPG keys. In an era of repudiation threats, every commit must be tied to a hardware-backed biometric token. If the developer didn't physically touch a key, the code doesn't enter the pipeline. This is your only defense against "AI-masked" malicious commits.
*   **Deploy "Logic-Diffing" Linters:** Standard SAST tools are failing to catch the logic-state collisions in Rust and Go. You need to implement semantic analysis tools (like modernized Semgrep or specialized Rust-logic checkers) that focus on *state transitions* rather than just syntax.
*   **Audit the "AI-Generated" Ratio:** Start tagging code in your repositories based on its origin. Use telemetry to identify which modules were 80%+ generated by AI. These modules require a "Human-in-the-Loop" (HITL) deep audit before being promoted to production-critical status. Treat AI-generated code as "untrusted third-party library" code by default.

#### 2. Long-Term Strategy (The Pivot)

*   **Shift from "Vulnerability Management" to "Digital Provenance":** The long-term winner won't be the CISO with the fewest CVEs, but the one who can provide a complete, immutable chain of custody for every line of code. This involves moving toward **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM) 2.0**, which includes not just libraries, but the *identity of the LLM* and the *identity of the human* who approved the prompt.
*   **Architect for "Blast-Radius Isolation" (The Cell Model):** Since we can no longer guarantee the logical integrity of "memory-safe" code, we must assume it will fail. Move away from flat microservices toward a "Cell-Based Architecture" where each functional unit has its own identity, its own data store, and zero-trust communication protocols. If a Rust-based logic error allows an escape in one cell, the rest of the organism remains uncompromised.
*   **Invest in "Adversarial AI Red-Teaming":** If your red team is still manually testing XSS, they are obsolete. You need to pivot your security testing budget toward **Automated Adversarial Simulation**. Use LLMs to attack your LLMs. The goal is to find the "slop-holes"—the areas where AI-generated code has created non-deterministic paths that a human auditor would never think to check.

The May 2026 roundup isn't a warning of a coming storm; it's a report from the middle of it. The organizations that thrive will be those that stop seeking "perfect" code and start building systems that are **verifiable, attributable, and resilient to their own complexity.**

### Technical Analysis: What's Really Happening

### The Mechanic: What's Actually Happening

When we talk about "Secure by Design" (SbD) in the closing weeks of 2025 and the dawn of 2026, we aren't just talking about better coding standards. We are witnessing a collision between the **normalization of deviance** and the cold reality of modern kinetic-digital threats. For years, the industry has operated on a "ship now, patch later" mentality that has effectively baked vulnerability into the very crust of our digital infrastructure. I’ve watched this play out in dozens of post-mortems: a developer bypasses a security control to meet a sprint deadline, the sky doesn't fall, and suddenly, that bypass becomes the new standard operating procedure. 

This "normalization of deviance"—a term famously coined following the Challenger disaster—is the silent killer of enterprise security. In the context of the Dec/Jan 2026 landscape, this manifests as the persistent use of memory-unsafe languages in critical paths and the "temporary" hardcoding of credentials in CI/CD pipelines that remain for years. We are seeing a technical debt crisis that has reached its interest-only payment phase. The "Mechanic" here isn't a single exploit; it’s the **architectural erosion** caused by thousands of small, "justifiable" compromises in the name of velocity.

Furthermore, the threat modeling news mentioned in the roundup signals a shift toward **Continuous Threat Modeling (CTM)**. We are moving away from the static, "once-a-year" PDF that sits in a GRC tool, gathering digital dust. The technical reality now involves integrating threat modeling directly into the IDE and the build pipeline. We are seeing tools that can programmatically flag when a code change violates the established threat model—for instance, when a new microservice suddenly requires outbound internet access without a documented justification. This isn't just "shifting left"; it's embedding the security architect's brain into the automated workflow.

Finally, we have to address the "GPS vs. Regulation" debate. While the C-suite is currently obsessed with the looming shadow of personal liability and regulatory fines, the actual technical threat landscape is being reshaped by **electronic warfare (EW) techniques** migrating into the commercial sector. GPS spoofing and jamming are no longer just "battlefield problems." They are becoming "supply chain problems." If your automated warehouse or your synchronized financial ledger relies on a GPS-disciplined oscillator for timing, a localized GPS attack is more catastrophic than any GDPR fine. The "threat model" is expanding beyond the packet and into the physics of the environment.

### The "So What?": Why This Matters

Why should a CISO care about the "normalization of deviance" more than the latest zero-day? Because zero-days are expensive for attackers to find, but **deviance is free for them to exploit.** When we normalize insecure defaults, we lower the "Cost per Pwn" for our adversaries. We are essentially building glass houses and handing out stones. This breaks the unified security model because it creates a "shadow architecture" where the documented security controls bear no resemblance to the actual data flows.

The shift in threat modeling matters because it exposes the **delusion of compliance.** For too long, organizations have equated "being compliant" with "being secure." The roundup suggests that regulatory threats—while terrifying to the legal department—don't actually change the *technical* threat model as much as physical disruptions like GPS attacks. This is a critical distinction. A regulation might change *how you report* a breach, but it doesn't change *how the breach happens.* 

If we ignore the physical-layer threats (like GPS interference) in favor of chasing the latest regulatory checkbox, we leave the back door wide open. We are seeing the emergence of **"Asymmetric Infrastructure Risk."** An attacker doesn't need to bypass your $2M firewall if they can simply spoof the time-sync on your authentication server, causing every Kerberos ticket in the environment to expire simultaneously. This is the "So What?": our threat models have been too focused on the application layer while the foundation—the physical and temporal reality our systems inhabit—is being subverted.

Moreover, the "Secure by Design" movement is finally putting a price tag on **vendor negligence.** The narrative is shifting from "the user failed to patch" to "the vendor failed to build." This matters because it changes the insurance and liability landscape. If you are a Security Architect today, you aren't just evaluating features; you are evaluating the **security pedigree** of your vendors. You are looking for those who have signed the CISA "Secure by Design" pledge and, more importantly, those who can prove they have eradicated entire classes of vulnerabilities (like SQL injection or buffer overflows) from their product lines.

### Strategic Defense: What To Do About It

To counter the normalization of deviance and adapt to the evolving threat model, we need a bifurcated approach that addresses both the immediate rot and the long-term architectural vision.

#### 1. Immediate Actions (Tactical Response)

*   **Audit the "Exceptions" List:** Every CISO has a list of "temporary" security exceptions. **Review every exception older than 90 days.** If it hasn't been remediated, it is no longer an exception; it is a normalized deviance. Force a re-justification or a hard kill-date. This is the only way to stop the "Challenger" moment in your SOC.
*   **Implement "Time-Source Diversity":** Given the rise in GPS-based attacks, move away from a single point of failure for NTP (Network Time Protocol). Implement **multi-source time synchronization** that includes PTP (Precision Time Protocol) and terrestrial-based atomic clocks where possible. If your logs or auth systems lose sync, your security posture collapses.
*   **Deploy Automated SBOM Analysis:** Don't just collect Software Bills of Materials (SBOMs); **automate the analysis of them.** Use tools like *Dependency-Track* or *Snyk* to cross-reference your SBOMs against the CISA KEV (Known Exploited Vulnerabilities) list daily. If a vendor provides a product with a known "Secure by Design" failure (like a hardcoded root password), trigger an immediate procurement review.

#### 2. Long-Term Strategy (The Pivot)

*   **The "Memory Safety" Mandate:** Establish a 3-year roadmap to migrate all "critical-path" internal development to **memory-safe languages (Rust, Go, or Swift).** This isn't just a technical preference; it is a strategic move to eliminate 70% of the vulnerability classes that plague modern software. Stop trying to "patch" your way out of C++ memory leaks and start "designing" your way out.
*   **Adopt "Policy as Code" for Threat Modeling:** Move threat modeling out of Word documents and into **HCL (HashiCorp Configuration Language) or Open Policy Agent (OPA).** Define your threat boundaries and security requirements as code that is tested during every CI/CD run. If a developer attempts to deploy a resource that violates the "Secure by Design" blueprint (e.g., an S3 bucket with public read access or a service without mTLS), the build must fail automatically.
*   **Resilience Testing (Chaos Security Engineering):** Stop assuming your controls work. Introduce **Security Chaos Engineering** into your production-like environments. Purposely desync the time, inject latency into your identity provider, or simulate a localized GPS outage. Observe how your systems fail. A "Secure by Design" system should fail gracefully and securely, not catastrophically and silently.

**Final Thought:** The "Secure by Design" roundup isn't a call for more tools; it's a call for a **cultural reckoning.** We have to stop accepting "good enough" as a standard. The organizations that survive the next five years will be those that treat security not as a layer to be added, but as a fundamental property of the system—as essential as uptime and as non-negotiable as the laws of physics.

## Article 3: Akira Ransomware Uses Safe Mode to Bypass EDR

### Technical Analysis: What's Really Happening

### The Mechanic: What's Actually Happening

We have spent the last decade building a fortress of "Next-Gen" security. We’ve poured billions into EDR, XDR, and AI-driven behavioral analytics, all predicated on a single assumption: that the security stack will be awake and watching when the thief climbs through the window. But the recent Akira ransomware campaign reveals a sobering reality. The attackers didn't try to outsmart the EDR; they simply asked the operating system to turn it off.

The technique is as elegant as it is frustratingly simple. After gaining initial access—in this case, through a **SonicWall VPN left wide open without Multi-Factor Authentication (MFA)**—the Akira affiliate didn't immediately deploy their payload. Instead, they performed the digital equivalent of a casing operation, harvesting credentials and mapping file shares. Once they had the keys to the kingdom, they executed a command that should trigger alarms in every SOC in the country: they forced the compromised host to reboot into **Safe Mode with Networking.**

Why Safe Mode? Because Safe Mode is the "diagnostic" state of Windows, designed to load only the bare essentials. Most EDR and AV agents are designed to be "good citizens" of the OS; they don't load their drivers in Safe Mode to avoid causing boot loops or interfering with system repairs. By forcing a reboot into this state, Akira effectively stripped the system of its armor. They turned a high-tech battlefield into a dark alley where the security cameras were unplugged. Once in Safe Mode, they used their elevated privileges to kill any lingering security services that might have had "Safe Mode" persistence, then attempted to launch the encryptor. 

There is a dark irony in the conclusion of this specific incident: the Akira encryptor failed not because of a brilliant defensive maneuver, but because of **memory allocation errors.** The attackers were so focused on bypassing the EDR that they didn't account for the resource constraints of the Safe Mode environment. We were saved by the attacker's poor coding, not our own vigilance. Relying on an adversary’s incompetence is not a strategy; it is a stay of execution.

### The "So What?": Why This Matters

This isn't just another ransomware story; it’s a post-mortem on the "Single Point of Failure" architecture that still dominates enterprise security. When we look at the Akira incident, we see three critical failures that should keep any CISO awake at night.

First, it exposes the **fragility of the EDR-centric model.** If your entire detection strategy relies on an agent running in a standard user-mode or kernel-mode environment, you have a blind spot the size of a freight truck. The "Safe Mode" trick has been known for years—Sophos and Mandiant have reported on it previously—yet it remains a highly effective "skeleton key." It lowers the barrier to entry for affiliates who don't have the skills to write custom obfuscators or zero-day exploits. Why spend months developing a bypass when you can just use `bcdedit /set {current} safeboot network`?

Second, the **MFA-less VPN** remains the industry’s "Original Sin." In 2026, seeing a SonicWall VPN without MFA as an entry point is like finding out a bank left its vault open because the manager forgot his keys. Akira, like many ransomware-as-a-service (RaaS) groups, thrives on the low-hanging fruit. This incident proves that even as we talk about "Zero Trust" and "AI-driven defense," the basics are still being ignored. An attacker doesn't need to be a genius if the front door is unlocked.

Third, this attack highlights the **asymmetry of the "Reboot."** In a modern, distributed enterprise, a server rebooting unexpectedly is often treated as a "glitch" or a "performance issue" rather than a Tier-1 security event. Akira exploited the fact that most IT operations teams prioritize uptime over forensic integrity. By the time someone realized the server wasn't just "acting up" but was actually being systematically stripped of its defenses in Safe Mode, the data was already being staged for exfiltration. This is a failure of **contextual monitoring**—we are watching the processes, but we aren't watching the state of the machine itself.

### Strategic Defense: What To Do About It

To defeat this tactic, we must stop treating the operating system as a trusted partner and start treating it as a potential accomplice. If an attacker can manipulate the boot configuration, they own the narrative. Here is how we reclaim it.

#### 1. Immediate Actions (Tactical Response)

*   **Enforce Hardware-Level Integrity:** Enable **UEFI Secure Boot** and **Trusted Platform Module (TPM)** requirements across all endpoints and servers. This makes it significantly harder for an attacker to persist in non-standard boot environments or tamper with the bootloader.
*   **Monitor and Alert on Boot Configuration Changes:** Set up immediate, high-priority alerts for any use of the `bcdedit` command, especially those involving the `safeboot` flag. There is almost no legitimate reason for a production server to be put into Safe Mode remotely during business hours. This should be treated as an "Indicator of Compromise" (IoC) in real-time.
*   **Kill the MFA-less Perimeter:** Audit every single entry point—VPNs, RDP gateways, SaaS applications. If it doesn't have MFA, it doesn't exist on your network. Specifically for SonicWall or similar edge devices, ensure they are patched against known vulnerabilities (like the recent CVEs affecting SMA 100 series) and that "Management Access" is restricted to internal IPs only.
*   **EDR Persistence Checks:** Work with your EDR vendor to determine if their agent can be configured to run in Safe Mode. Some modern agents now have a "Safe Mode" protection driver that can be enabled, though it is often disabled by default to prevent stability issues. Turn it on for your "Crown Jewel" assets.

#### 2. Long-Term Strategy (The Pivot)

*   **Transition to "Attestation-Based" Access:** Move away from simple VPNs toward a **Zero Trust Network Access (ZTNA)** model where the "health" of the device is checked before access is granted. If a device’s boot configuration has been altered or if its EDR agent is heartbeat-silent, the network should automatically revoke its access to file shares and internal resources. The machine should be isolated at the network layer, regardless of what mode the OS is running in.
*   **Immutable Backup Architecture:** Akira’s goal is to encrypt your data to force a ransom. If your backups are stored on a standard Windows file share that the attacker can reach after their "Safe Mode" bypass, your backups are as good as gone. Shift to **immutable, off-site, or "air-gapped" cloud backups** that require multi-party authorization to delete or modify.
*   **Behavioral Identity Analytics:** Since Akira spent time harvesting credentials before the reboot, the real opportunity to catch them was during the **Lateral Movement** phase. Implement identity-centric monitoring that flags unusual service account usage or "impossible travel" logins. We need to catch the thief while they are casing the house, not while they are driving the getaway truck.

The Akira incident is a reminder that in cybersecurity, the "old" tricks never really die—they just wait for us to become complacent. Safe Mode is a feature, not a bug, but in the hands of an affiliate with a SonicWall credential, it is a weapon. It’s time we stop building higher walls and start ensuring that the ground they are built on can't be shifted beneath our feet.
